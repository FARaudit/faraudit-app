The CMMC pause is being executed one solicitation at a time — and the amendments are not just deleting a requirement. At least one of them reopened a competition that had already closed.

On 13 July the Department of War suspended CMMC Phase II — the third-party certification requirement set to become a condition of award on 10 November.

Every alert since has said the same true thing: the assessment is paused, the obligation is not. DFARS 252.204-7012 still binds you. NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 is still the enforced standard. Phase I self-assessments still apply.

That's correct. It also isn't the part that costs you money this month.

## What the memo actually directs

Read the implementation memo — 26-P-1023, Attachment 1 — and it says something more specific than "paused."

During the suspension, program managers and requiring activities may designate only CMMC Level 1 (Self) or Level 2 (Self). They may not designate Level 2 (C3PAO) or Level 3 (DIBCAC). Where a live solicitation already carries one of those designations, the requiring activity must initiate an amendment removing it, and the contracting officer must issue that amendment as soon as practicable. Existing contracts get it stripped by modification before the next option exercise or the next administrative mod. And no waivers are granted while the review runs.

Most of the coverage stopped there, at the policy. Here is what it looks like when it lands on a real solicitation.

## One real example

NAVFAC Southwest, notice N6247326RE001 — Facility Planning Services A-E IDIQ. Architectural services, San Diego, total small business set-aside.

The amendment did four things. It revised the CMMC level required at time of contract award from Level 2 (C3PAO) to Level 2 Self-Assessment. It made the same revision to the level specified in provision 252.204-7025. It reopened the solicitation. And it moved the SF330 package due date from 16 June to 18 August — then a second amendment, posted 31 July, moved it again to 2 September.

Read that sequence again, because the last part is what matters.

The requirement didn't just get softer. The competition reopened, and the deadline moved out by two and a half months.

If you looked at that solicitation in May, saw a C3PAO certification you don't hold, and moved on — which was the correct commercial decision on the information you had — then as of today there is a live bid you don't know about. Nobody emailed you. The amendment posted to a notice you had already closed the tab on.

That's one solicitation. A keyword search of active notices on SAM.gov returns twenty-one that mention C3PAO right now. I opened two of them. I'd suggest you open the ones carrying your NAICS code.

## Three things to do this week

1. Go back through what you declined. Not what you're working — what you passed on since roughly March on cybersecurity-certification grounds. Check whether it's been amended, and whether the date moved.

2. On anything live, search the text for "C3PAO" and "DIBCAC," and check provision 252.204-7025 specifically. If either appears, ask the contracting officer in writing, inside the question window: given the CIO memorandum of 13 July 2026, will this solicitation be amended to remove the Level 2 (C3PAO) designation? You're not arguing. You're asking a question that now has a clear answer.

3. Get your primes' position in writing. The memo directs government program managers, requiring activities and contracting officers. It does not rewrite what a prime chose to flow down in a subcontract you already signed. Silence from a prime is not relief.

## And the part almost nobody will do

The Department isn't only pausing. It's asking what should replace this.

The RFI — Reforming CMMC and Reducing Compliance Burden for the Defense Industrial Base — poses seven questions. The first asks you to identify the top five most prohibitive cost drivers, administrative burdens or operational challenges you've experienced or expect to. Another asks which controls delivered real risk reduction, and which produced the highest administrative and financial burden with the least measurable improvement to your actual security posture.

That is a government asking small and mid-size contractors to submit the cost data that will shape the rule they are then held to.

Responses are due at 12:00 p.m. Eastern on Friday, 14 August. Email only — the addresses and the format requirements are in the RFI document attached to the notice. The task force reports to the CIO around mid-September.

Eleven days.

The companies with compliance departments will file, and they'll file with their numbers. If the firms carrying the heaviest relative burden — the machine shops, the small engineering houses, the eight-person specialists — don't put their own numbers into the record, the replacement gets designed around someone else's cost structure.

You don't need a lawyer to answer question one. You need your own invoices.

## Why this one matters to me

I spent eight years inside Lockheed Martin program finance. I built the estimates, ran the EACs, and watched what happens to a program's margin when a requirement moves after the number is locked.

The lesson I took from that isn't about cybersecurity. It's that requirements move constantly, the movement is always documented somewhere public, and almost nobody goes back to re-read a document they have already made a decision about. The decision gets made once, on the version you happened to read.

This month there is a solicitation in San Diego that proves the point, and an eleven-day window where the government is asking for your numbers.

Go read the memo. Then go back through what you declined.
